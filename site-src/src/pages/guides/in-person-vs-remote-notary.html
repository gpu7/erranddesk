In-person or remote online notary: how to choose (2026)

This page is the choice between meeting a notary in the same place and using remote online notarization. Where to go, what a notary may charge, and free options are already covered in notary options, free notary, and mobile notary fees.

Short version: A notarization generally requires the signer to appear before the notary and the notary to check identity [1]. Remote online notarization is one way some states let that appearance happen by live audio-video, with the people in different places [1]. An electronic file is not automatically remote. A notary who drives to you is still an in-person meeting. Your state, and the person who will receive the document, both have a say.

Not legal advice. Notary law is state law. This page compares official descriptions. It does not say a particular document will be accepted, and it is not a recommendation to use remote notarization. Errand Desk isn't affiliated with any secretary of state, library, or notary platform.

What does "in person" mean?

The National Association of Secretaries of State says state notary laws vary, and that the shared pieces are personal appearance of the signer and verification of identity. For a long time that appearance meant the signer was physically with the notary. Around 2000, states and federal law started allowing notaries to use an electronic signature. That change is about the signature and the record. It is not, by itself, a remote meeting [1].

A notary who travels to your home or office is still in the same place as you. Travel fees, where a state allows them, are in the mobile notary guide. Travel is not remote online notarization.

What changes with remote online notarization?

NASS describes remote electronic notarization as a later step: under the laws that allow it, personal appearance can be satisfied by audio-video technology while the notary and the signer are in different places. NASS adopted standards for states that want them. It says those standards are not an endorsement of electronic or remote notarization, and that NASS does not take a position on whether states should allow either method. States can adopt one, both, or neither. Requirements and procedures vary [1].

As of this check, NASS says 47 states and the District of Columbia have a law that allows remote e-notarization. That count is a snapshot, not a promise that every notarization in those states can be done remotely, or that a given recipient will accept one [1].

How do three states describe it?

These are examples from the pages below. They are not a national rule.

  • Florida: "Appear before" means either physical presence, or being outside that person's physical presence while able to see, hear, and communicate by audio-video technology [2]. An online notary physically located in Florida may perform an online notarization that meets the statute even if the principal or witnesses are not physically in Florida [3]. The fee for the online notarial act itself may not exceed $25. Fees for other services, including a remote online notarization service provider, are not governed by that fee section [4]. That is a cap on one charge, not a price list for platforms.
  • Texas: The Secretary of State's online-notary training says an online notary must already be a commissioned Texas notary, and conducts the notarization in a secure two-way audio-visual conference. The same lesson says the notary must be physically located in Texas and the signer can be anywhere in the world. It points to Government Code chapter 406, Civil Practice and Remedies Code chapter 121, and Administrative Code chapter 87 [5]. NASS also lists Texas among the states that authorized remote electronic notarization [1]. A page that only explains traditional in-person appearance is not a statement that Texas lacks remote notarization.
  • California: The Secretary of State's notary handbook FAQ says current California law does not authorize California notaries to perform remote online notarization by audio-video communication. The signer must be physically present. A video image is not personal appearance under current California law. The same handbook says the remaining remote-notarization provisions become operative when the Secretary of State finishes its technology project or on January 1, 2030, whichever is earlier [6]. That is California. It is not a description of Texas or Florida.

Will the recipient accept it?

A statute that allows a remote notarization does not decide whether a bank, court, recorder, employer, or government agency will accept that document. Ask the recipient before you book a session. This page does not claim that passports, real-estate filings, or any other document type can be notarized remotely.

Does the certificate change how you sign?

The wording on the notarial certificate matters, and states write it differently. Two official descriptions of the common split:

  • California handbook: an acknowledgment certifies that the signer personally appeared, that the notary identified the signer, and that the signer acknowledged executing the document. A jurat certifies that the signer personally appeared, signed in the notary's presence, and took an oath or affirmation. A jurat cannot be attached to a document the signer did not sign in front of the notary [6].
  • Texas Secretary of State, traditional notary training: for an acknowledgment, the signer may bring a document that is already signed, but must still appear and acknowledge the signature. A jurat must be sworn and signed in the notary's presence. The lesson says not to notarize a jurat if the signer does not sign in front of you [7]. Texas online sessions use audio-visual appearance instead of standing in the same room [5]. The certificate type still tells you whether the signer is acknowledging an existing signature or signing under oath during the session.

Read the certificate on your form. If it says "subscribed and sworn," ask the notary before you sign ahead of time.

Which kind of witness is this?

An identifying witness and a witness the document asks for are different jobs.

California's handbook uses two different names. A credible witness is one way to prove who the signer is, by oath, when the notary is identifying the signer. A subscribing witness is someone who appears in place of a signer who does not appear, to prove that the signer executed the document. California limits which documents can use a subscribing-witness proof [6]. Those are California terms. Another state may not offer the same procedure.

A library page can ask for both kinds without using those names. The Laramie County Library in Cheyenne says that if you have no photo ID, you may bring two people with valid ID who can verify your identity. Separately, it says to call ahead if the document requires more than one witness [8]. The first sentence is about proving who you are. The second is about witnesses the document itself requires.

Two in-person library examples

These libraries describe a notary at the building. Their pages do not describe remote online notarization, and this guide does not treat them as remote-notary providers.

  • Cheyenne, Laramie County Library: the main library "offers free notary services during regular library hours." Bring a government-issued photo ID, or two people with valid ID who can verify your identity. Call 307-634-3561 to be sure a notary is on duty [8].
  • Duluth Public Library: notary service at the Main Library, 520 W. Superior St., Monday through Friday, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., depending on staff. Walk-ins may be taken. The library says service "is not guaranteed," so call 218-730-4221 first. Bring the unsigned document and a government-issued ID (tribal IDs are valid), or a witness who knows you and has ID. Staff cannot serve as witnesses for identification purposes. Staff will not notarize wills, birth, death, or marriage certificates, trusts, gun sales, deeds, titles, I-9 forms, depositions, real estate transactions, documents that require verification of facts, or documents that are not in English [9]. The page does not list a fee. This guide does not call the service free.

FAQ

Is an electronic document a remote notarization?

No. Electronic signatures on notarial acts and remote audio-video appearance are different steps. NASS describes them separately and says a state might allow one, both, or neither [1].

Is a mobile notary a remote notary?

No. A notary who travels to you meets you in person. Remote online notarization, as NASS describes it, puts the notary and signer in different places and connects them by audio-video [1]. Travel fees are in the mobile notary guide.

Can I use remote online notarization in every state?

No. NASS says requirements vary, and its count of state laws is not an endorsement or a guarantee that your document qualifies [1]. California's handbook says California notaries cannot perform remote online notarization under current law [6]. Florida and Texas describe their own remote procedures [2][3][5].

Do I sign before I see the notary?

It depends on the certificate and the state. California's handbook says a jurat is signed in the notary's presence. An acknowledgment is the signer acknowledging that they executed the document [6]. Texas's traditional-notary lesson says an acknowledgment may already be signed, and a jurat may not [7].

Is the Duluth library notary free or remote?

The library's page does not list a fee, and this guide does not call it free. The service it describes is at the Main Library, in person, when a notary is available. It does not describe remote online notarization [9].
