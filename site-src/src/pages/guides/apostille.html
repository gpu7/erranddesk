How to get an apostille: who issues it and what states charge (2026)

An apostille certifies a US public document so it can be used in another country. Who issues it depends on who issued your document: your state for state documents, and the U.S. Department of State for federal ones. This page uses only official government pages.

Short version: A state-issued document, such as a birth certificate or a notarized document, goes to the state first. If the destination country is in the 1961 Hague Convention, the state's certification is enough: "You do not need an apostille certificate" from the federal government [2]. For a country outside the Convention, you get a state authentication, then send it to the U.S. Department of State with Form DS-4194 [3]. The federal fee is $20 per document [4].

Not legal advice. This page is general information. Whoever asked for your document decides what they'll accept, so ask them which certification they need. Errand Desk isn't affiliated with the U.S. Department of State, any state office or any other government agency.

What is an apostille, and what does it certify?

"Countries that are in the 1961 Hague Convention Treaty may require an apostille certificate" [2]. "Countries not in the 1961 Hague Convention Treaty require authentication certificates to prove your documents are valid" [3]. Either way, the certificate is about the official, not your document's contents: "Authentication acknowledges the seal and signature of the official and not the document itself" [3].

How do I get an apostille on a birth certificate or other state document?

Send it to the state that issued it, such as the secretary of state, the lieutenant governor or another authority [3]. The U.S. Department of State says that for a Hague Convention country, "Get your document certified by the state who issued it ... You do not need an apostille certificate" [2]. The state table below links each state's official page and the fee it lists.

State pages often set extra steps. Some examples from the pages we checked: New York says vital records issued by a local or county official must first be certified by the County Clerk [37]. New Hampshire won't apostille a vital record that isn't submitted in the proper form [34]. Check your state's page before you mail anything.

How do I get an apostille on an FBI background check or other federal document?

The U.S. Department of State's Office of Authentications handles documents signed by a federal official, such as FBI background checks, DHS naturalization certificates, IRS tax residency certifications and USPTO documents [2]. "Do not notarize your original document. Your document will no longer be valid if it is notarized" [2].

  • Fee: "A $20 nonrefundable fee for each document" [4].
  • Form: DS-4194 [4].
  • By mail: "We will process it within 5 weeks from the day we receive it." Pay by "check or money order made out to the U.S. Department of State." Mail to Office of Authentications, U.S. Department of State, 44132 Mercure Circle, PO Box 1206, Sterling, VA 20166-1206 [4].
  • Walk-in, if you travel in 2-3 weeks: processed within 7 business days, at 600 19th Street NW, Washington, D.C. 20006, 7:30 to 9:00 a.m., Monday to Thursday. In person, pay by credit or debit card or contactless payment; cash, checks and money orders aren't accepted [4].
  • Family emergency, traveling within 2 weeks: same-day appointment [4].

What if the country isn't in the Hague Convention?

You need an authentication certificate instead of an apostille. For a state document, "Get your document authenticated by the state who issued it" first, then submit it to the U.S. Department of State with Form DS-4194 and the $20 per-document fee [3][4]. Whether the destination country needs anything after that is not stated on the federal pages we used, so ask whoever requested the document.

What to check before you send anything

  • Ask which certification the recipient wants, and confirm the destination country. Hague and non-Hague countries need different certificates [2][3].
  • Match the office to the document. State documents go to the state that issued them, and federal documents go to the U.S. Department of State [2][3].
  • Don't notarize a federal original [2]. For state documents that need a notary first, see our notary guide.
  • Keep copies. See our photocopy guide, and the shipping guide for tracked mail.

FAQ

Do I need the U.S. Department of State to apostille my birth certificate?

Not for a Hague Convention country. Get it certified by the state that issued it. The U.S. Department of State says, "You do not need an apostille certificate" [2].

How much does a federal apostille cost?

The U.S. Department of State charges a $20 nonrefundable fee for each document [4].

How long does a federal apostille take by mail?

The U.S. Department of State says it will process a mailed request within 5 weeks from the day it receives it [4].

What is the difference between an apostille and an authentication?

An apostille is for countries in the 1961 Hague Convention. An authentication certificate is for countries outside it, and a state document then also goes to the U.S. Department of State [2][3].

Does an apostille prove my document is true?

No. "Authentication does not verify the contents of a document." It acknowledges the official's seal and signature [3].
