Mobile notary fees by state: what the law caps (2026)

A mobile notary is a notary public who comes to you, at home, at work, at a hospital or anywhere else you arrange. State law usually caps the notary fee for each act or signature. Travel is a separate charge, and states handle it differently. Here is the cap and any travel-fee rule for every state and D.C., taken only from statutes and state pages.

Short version: The notary fee itself is capped in most states, often at $2 to $15 per act or signature, for example $10 per act in Florida [11] and $15 per signature for an acknowledgment in California [5]. The travel fee is a separate charge. Many states allow it only if you agree to it before the notary travels, and a few cap it, for example at the IRS mileage rate in Connecticut and Wyoming [7][53]. Ask for the notary fee and the travel fee separately, in writing, before you book.

Not legal advice. This page is general information from official state sources. Fee laws change, so check the official page for your state before you rely on a figure. Errand Desk isn't affiliated with any government agency or notary service.

What is a mobile notary?

It's a commissioned notary public who travels to the signer instead of having the signer come to an office. The notarial act is the same one you'd get at a bank or a shipping store. What changes is the bill: you pay the notary fee allowed by your state, plus any travel fee you agree to. Several states describe that travel fee in their notary laws, for example North Carolina allows "actual mileage at the federal business mileage rate if the travel reimbursement is agreed to by the principal in writing prior to the travel" [36].

A mobile notary is different from remote online notarization, where you meet a notary over video. For online options and in-person places that may be free, see where to get something notarized and free notary options.

How do travel fees work?

Where state law addresses travel, the common rules in the sources above are:

  • Agree in advance. Missouri, New Mexico, North Dakota, Oregon, South Carolina, Utah, Washington and Wyoming require the travel fee to be agreed before the travel, and the notary has to explain that it's separate from the notary fee and not set by law [27][34][37][40][43][47][50][53].
  • A cap tied to mileage rates. Connecticut and Wyoming use the IRS standard mileage rate, North Carolina and Utah use the federal mileage rate, Indiana uses federal travel rates set by the General Services Administration, and Maryland allows the IRS rate plus up to $5 [7][53][36][47][16][22].
  • An hourly cap. Nevada allows up to $15 an hour from 6 a.m. to 7 p.m. and $30 an hour from 7 p.m. to 6 a.m., only if you asked the notary to travel [30].
  • Itemize it. Pennsylvania treats travel as a clerical fee that must be disclosed before notarizing and itemized on the receipt, and California says travel fees should be identified separately in the notary's journal [41][5].

What should I ask before booking a mobile notary?

  • The notary fee for each signature or act, and how many acts your documents need.
  • The travel fee, in writing, and whether it's a flat amount, mileage or hourly.
  • What ID the signer needs to bring, and whether any witnesses are needed.
  • Whether the document needs anything after notarization, such as an apostille for use abroad.

Compare the quote with the cap in the table. If a notary fee is above your state's cap, ask the notary to explain it, or check with your state's notary office.

Mobile notary FAQ

Is a mobile notary fee the same as the travel fee?

No. The notary fee is for the notarial act and is capped in most states. The travel fee is a separate charge for coming to you. States that address it usually require you to agree to it in advance, and some cap it [40][53].

Can a notary charge whatever they want for travel?

It depends on the state. Some cap it, for example at the IRS standard mileage rate in Connecticut and Wyoming, or $15 to $30 an hour in Nevada [7][53][30]. Others only require that it be agreed in advance. Where our source doesn't state a rule, check with your state's notary office.

Which states have no fixed maximum notary fee?

In the sources we checked, Alaska, Arkansas, Iowa, Kansas, Louisiana, Maine, South Dakota and Tennessee don't set a dollar maximum, though several require the fee to be reasonable or disclosed before the act [2][4][17][18][20][54][44][45].

Is the fee per signature or per document?

It varies. California, Missouri, South Carolina, Utah and West Virginia count per signature for some acts, Colorado counts per document, and Ohio says its fees "shall not be calculated on a per signature basis" [5][27][43][47][51][6][38].

Can I get something notarized for free instead?

Often, yes, if you can travel. Many banks and credit unions notarize for customers at no charge. See free notary options.
